The effect of the AI boom on the environment

3 October 2026

The US is one of the foremost AI powers, rivalled only, possibly, by the People's Republic of China. With AI powerhouses such as Anthropic and OpenAI investing millions of dollars into singular projects, for example the recent mathematical breakthrough of the Navier-Stokes problem was estimated to cost OpenAI around $15 million, with most of the cost made up by the 10,000 AI agents running constantly for 88 hours. The problem is that this power to run the billions of queries that the chatbots such as Claude and ChatGPT receive every day, along with the research that they complete, has to come from somewhere. It is estimated that AI companies use 400 to 415 TWh annually (about 1.5% of total global electricity demand), matching the yearly electricity consumption of entire medium-sized nations like the Netherlands.

The most important factor with this energy use however is where it comes from; AI data centres are popping up all over the US, with OpenAI's planned Project Stargate in Abilene, Texas, projected at 8 million square feet. For context the average US home is about 2,400 square feet, meaning you could fit roughly 3,333 homes on the same plot of land. Is there a limit to the size of these mega-centres? Currently there are no US laws limiting the physical or energy size of AI data centres, meaning that they can utilize as much energy as they please. However a new bill has been proposed by Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, this proposed bill (S.4214) seeks a federal pause on constructing or upgrading large AI data centres until strict national standards on safety, environment, and utility rates are enacted. These environmental protections would most importantly in my opinion limit the water usage of AI firms, which typically use 2 million to 19 million litres of water daily depending on their size, climate, and cooling technology.

The water problem

The scale of this water usage is difficult to comprehend until you put it in human terms. A single large data centre can drink more water in a day than a small town, and much of that water is drawn from the same municipal supplies that serve American households. In drought-prone states like Arizona, Texas, and California, this is not an abstract concern; it is a direct competition between the servers and the people. Farmers are already being asked to cut back on irrigation while data centres nearby are granted long-term water contracts, and in some communities residents have seen their utility rates rise to help pay for the infrastructure that these mega-centres demand.

Who gets priority?

The question of priority is where this stops being an abstract environmental debate and becomes a very concrete one about who America's infrastructure actually serves. When a data centre is proposed in a drought-stricken county, it does not arrive as one customer among many; it arrives as the single largest water and electricity consumer the region has ever seen, and it arrives with lawyers, lobbyists, and the promise of tax revenue. Local governments, many of them cash-strapped, are then placed in an impossible position: approve the project and gain jobs and a tax base, or refuse it and watch the investment go to a neighbouring state that will say yes. In practice, the answer is almost always yes.
